# LLM Contribution Summarization in Software Projects

**Authors:** Rafael Corsi Ferrao, Fabio de Miranda (equal contribution), Diego Pavan Soler (Insper, Sao Paulo, Brazil)
**Date:** Submitted 23 May 2025 (arXiv preprint); an earlier 2-page version appeared at ACM SIGCSE Virtual Global Computing Education Conference 2024; the full paper was later published at an IEEE conference (IEEE Xplore document 11328654)
**Source:** [arXiv:2505.17710](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17710) (HTML: https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17710v1)
**Relevance to Slackr:** Directly on-topic and the closest empirical precedent to Slackr found so far, closer than TRACE ([[2025-10-trace-ai-assisted-collaborative-assessment]]). This is a deployed, real-classroom tool that mines Git repository history with PyDriller, uses an LLM to attribute and summarize each student's individual code contributions, and hands the resulting summary to both students and instructors, explicitly *not* as a grade but as evidence to support "social regulation" within the team. This is close to a working prior implementation of Slackr's core GitHub-evidence pipeline, including its specific failure modes.

## Summary

The paper is an experience report from a 3-week, full-time, industry-sponsored software sprint at Insper (a Brazilian university), run across two semesters in 2024 with 65 students total (28 in one semester, 37 in the other), organized into teams of 4-6. Instructors could not feasibly track every team's internal work distribution by hand, and existing mutual-evaluation tools like CATME (a questionnaire-based peer-rating instrument) suffered from two known problems the authors cite directly from prior literature: students dislike uneven contribution levels but are reluctant to "expose" underperforming peers, and questionnaire-based peer evaluation cannot provide continuous, real-time signal, only periodic snapshots.

The authors built a tool that clones each team's private GitHub Classroom repository, runs PyDriller to produce git-blame data isolating line-by-line authorship per file, then runs a chain of LLM agents (GPT-4o-mini for per-file and per-contribution analysis, GPT-4o for the final synthesis) to turn that raw blame data into a structured, prose summary of what each student did, at what estimated difficulty, and how it fits into the team's overall progress. The tool optionally also assigns each student a business-style role label (e.g. "Junior Backend Developer," "Documenter") based on their contribution pattern. Summaries were delivered to teams twice per sprint (end of week 2 and week 3), for a total of 130 individual summaries across both semesters, and both students and non-author instructors reviewed them for accuracy and gave structured feedback.

Critically, the paper frames the tool's purpose the same way Slackr frames its own: not to compute a grade or a verdict, but to produce an external, objective account of contribution that lets the team itself have the "uneven contribution" conversation without one member having to personally accuse another. The paper explicitly credits this design as "facilitating fairer discussions about task distribution... without placing the burden of evidence production on individual members," which is close to a direct restatement of Slackr's "evidence, not verdict" positioning.

## Key Findings

- **The tool reports genuine student work in large proportion, but is not fully reliable.** Across 130 summaries, the authors documented 8 instances of partial omission (real tasks the student did were missing from their summary alongside other tasks that were correctly captured) and 4 instances of total omission (a student's completed work was entirely absent from their report).
- **"Comment injection" is identified as a specific, named failure mode.** In one case, a feature was misattributed to the wrong student because that student had written a code comment describing the feature, while a different student had actually authored the commits implementing it; the LLM trusted the comment text over the actual commit authorship. The authors flag this as a risk category that will likely grow as LLM-based contribution analysis becomes more common, i.e. a student (deliberately or not) commenting in a way that shapes how an automated tool credits the work.
- **Automated systems systematically overweight low-effort, high-visibility artifacts.** Both students and instructors independently raised the same complaint: the tool tended to inflate the apparent importance of small or superficial tasks (writing a README, running a scaffolding generator, trivial code tweaks) relative to substantive feature work, which instructors said sometimes "misrepresented the actual contributions of some students."
- **Co-authorship and pair-programming are a blind spot.** Students specifically asked the tool to account for GitHub co-authorship tags, noting that contributions made during pair-programming sessions were sometimes dropped entirely because the tool attributes work to a single blamed author per line.
- **Automated role-labelling (Junior/Senior + functional role) was the most contested feature.** Students found the classification "partially arbitrary," not comparable across teams of different overall productivity (a "Junior Frontend Developer" on a strong team had objectively done more than a "Senior Frontend Developer" on a weaker team), and ultimately "confusing" rather than useful. This is a caution against Slackr ever exposing a similar auto-generated seniority/role label, as opposed to raw, comparative-free evidence.
- **The evidence changed behavior, not just perception.** Instructors observed that students with previously low participation increased their involvement after receiving the first round of feedback, and some students reported in interviews that being "seen" by the tool was itself motivating.
- **Cost was trivial.** Total LLM API cost for all summaries across an entire semester was about $4, which the authors present as evidence that frequent, near-real-time contribution summarization is economically practical at scale, not just a one-off research demo.
- **The grading design deliberately protected honest peer input.** In this course's separate CATME peer-evaluation component, students were graded on whether they *completed* the evaluation cycle, not on the content or valence of the ratings they gave, specifically to avoid disincentivizing honest feedback. This is a small but concrete precedent for decoupling "did you participate in the reporting process" from "what did you say," a pattern relevant to any future Slackr feature that asks teammates to confirm or add context to surfaced evidence.

## Notable Quotes

> "On one hand, they dislike the uneven levels of contributions among group members; on the other hand, they tend to avoid conflicts to address this issue and are reluctant to expose peers who are not contributing effectively."

> "The external summary of contributions facilitated social regulation within student teams, allowing groups to discuss and redistribute tasks more fairly without placing the burden of evidence production on individual members."

> "This may establish a category of 'comment injections', where a student writes an untrue comment in the code that the LLM accepts as accurate, should LLMs become more widespread for source code analysis."

> "I felt that I had done more than what was written. I made the password change function and all that, maybe in the final print it didn't appear, but I worked a lot." (student interview, on a partial-omission case)

> "...but it didn't make much sense when it said 'ah, it's junior, it's senior', and all that... I really didn't find it that viable." (student interview, on the auto-assigned role labels)

> "The Git log evaluation was automated, focusing on quantitative metrics such as commits and lines of code. However, this analysis did not consider the content of the changed lines of code or the type of file." (literature review, describing prior work by Buffardi, contrasted with this paper's content-aware approach)

## Why This Matters for Slackr

- This is the strongest available proof-of-concept that Slackr's core mechanic, automatically converting Git activity into a human-readable, per-member contribution account that stops short of a grade, works in a real classroom and is perceived as useful and fair by both students and instructors. It is direct empirical support for Slackr's design thesis, not just a conceptual analogue like TRACE ([[2025-10-trace-ai-assisted-collaborative-assessment]]).
- The two named failure modes, partial/total omission and "comment injection," are concrete, citable risks Slackr should design against if it ever moves from human-reviewed evidence toward LLM-generated narrative summaries of that evidence. They argue for keeping raw, verifiable evidence (commit lists, diffs, timestamps) alongside or instead of prose summaries, and for never letting free-text content (commit messages, code comments) override structural authorship data (blame, commit metadata) when attributing work.
- The finding that automated tools systematically over-credit low-effort, high-visibility changes (README edits, scaffolding) versus substantive work is a direct design warning for how Slackr should weight or present evidence: raw commit/line counts, or naive LLM summarization, both risk flattening "wrote one paragraph of documentation" and "implemented the authentication module" into equivalent-looking line items unless the evidence layer explicitly signals task substance.
- The pushback against auto-generated role/seniority labels is a strong argument for Slackr staying with neutral, descriptive evidence ("committed to auth.py, 40 lines, 3 commits") rather than any evaluative or comparative label ("Junior Backend Developer"), reinforcing the project's existing "evidence, not verdict" stance from a new angle: even a purely descriptive-sounding classification can be read as a verdict and generate the same fairness complaints a numeric score would.
- The co-authorship/pair-programming blind spot is a concrete edge case Slackr's own GitHub-evidence design should account for explicitly, since Slackr's identity-mapping and evidence-collection approach (per the MVP spec) is also fundamentally commit- and blame-based.
- The CATME grading detail (graded for completion of the evaluation cycle, not content of ratings) is a small, citable precedent for how to keep any future light peer-input feature in Slackr honest without exposing it to the effort/incentive problem formally proven in [[2026-05-peerbts-strategyproof-peer-selection]].
