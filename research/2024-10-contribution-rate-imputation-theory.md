# Contribution Rate Imputation Theory: A Conceptual Model

**Authors:** Vincil Bishop III, Steven Simske (Department of Systems Engineering, Colorado State University)
**Date:** Submitted October 11, 2024
**Source:** [arXiv:2410.09285](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09285) (PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.09285)
**Relevance to Slackr:** Directly on-topic for the GitHub side of Slackr's evidence pipeline. This paper is a full technical treatment of exactly the problem Slackr's MVP spec sidesteps on purpose: raw commit counts and lines of code do not represent true developer effort or contribution quality. It builds a mathematical method (CRIM) to try to fix that gap, and its own stated limitations are a strong, citable argument for why Slackr treats commit activity as evidence rather than a score.

## Summary

The paper introduces the "Contribution Rate Imputation Method" (CRIM), a conceptual model for estimating how many person-hours a developer likely spent on a code contribution, even when no direct time-tracking data exists. It builds on a prior "Time-Delta Method" (Bishop, 2024 PhD dissertation) that computes the elapsed time between a developer's consecutive commits (the "Commit Time Delta") and pairs it with a code-complexity measure (cyclomatic complexity or Levenshtein distance) to derive a "contribution rate": how much complexity/change a developer typically produces per hour. CRIM extends this by statistically imputing effort during long gaps between commits, where the raw time delta would otherwise overstate how long someone actually worked (e.g., a week between two commits does not mean a full week of continuous effort).

The central formula is simple: Δt = ΔL / ρ, where Δt is estimated hours spent, ΔL is the size/complexity of the contribution, and ρ is a "model contribution rate" — an empirically derived baseline for how fast a typical developer produces that kind of change. The paper proposes several ways to estimate ρ (e.g., Mean Bound Contribution Rate, regression models like Gradient Boosted Trees) and discusses adjusting it for developer experience, project phase, and task complexity.

## Key Findings and Quotes

- The paper's core motivation statement doubles as a direct citation for Slackr's own design stance: "Traditional metrics such as lines of code and commit counts fail to capture the true effort involved in producing meaningful contributions, often overlooking the complexity of the work and the actual time spent."
- The Contribution Rate Imputation Theory itself is stated as: "If the rate at which development commonly takes place is known, actual person hours for a software contribution can be estimated." This is explicitly framed as an *estimate/inference*, not a direct measurement: "This estimated time is not a direct measurement but rather an inference based on past behavior and contribution patterns."
- The authors are candid about the fragility of their own core variable, ρ (the model contribution rate): "the CRIM is incomplete and not practically applicable without an effective mechanism to accurately identify model contribution rate[s]." They list this as an open problem left "as an opportunity for future research," not something solved in this paper.
- They flag that contributions of similar size are not equivalent in effort: "a contribution of 100 lines of code that addresses a security vulnerability may demand more effort than a similar-sized contribution that adds a simple new feature," meaning ρ "may need to be adjusted for different types of contributions" — i.e., the model cannot currently tell "hard" work from "easy" work of the same size.
- Small datasets make the whole method unreliable: "Without sufficient historical data, the model contribution rate risks being skewed by outliers... leading to higher variability and lower confidence in the imputed values." A single student team's semester-long repo is exactly this kind of small, low-signal dataset.
- Even in its best case, the method admits it "does not account for unpredictable factors such as interruptions, meetings, or other non-development activities that may affect actual work time," and non-code work (code review, bug triage, documentation) is explicitly named as something CRIM does not currently capture at all, left as future work.
- Their literature review (Section 2.1) independently surveys ~9 prior approaches to "measuring developer contribution" (activity-level classification, repository mining metrics, PR-based metrics, quality metrics, ownership-vs-contribution alignment, social network analysis, entropy-based diversity metrics, and Git-metrics-for-student-teams specifically), and concludes contribution measurement is "a multifaceted challenge" requiring combinations of methods rather than any single metric.

## Why This Matters for Slackr

- This is a rare case of a paper that argues *for* going beyond commit counts, built by researchers who then hit, and openly document, the reasons that approach is hard to trust: an unsolved core parameter (ρ), no handling of non-code work, no correction for task difficulty vs. size, and unreliability on small datasets. That is precisely the situation a Slackr project repo would be in — a handful of students, one semester, incomplete history. This gives Slackr's product decision ("Commit count is evidence, not a measure of contribution quality," `slackr-mvp.md` §8) a well-documented technical justification rather than just a design intuition: the state of the art for converting commits into an effort *score* is explicitly unfinished, even by the people building it.
- It strengthens the case for Slackr's Member Context feature. CRIM has no mechanism for code review, bug triage, documentation, or any non-commit work; the authors name this as a known gap. Slackr's "Add Context" step is a direct, low-tech answer to a limitation this more sophisticated quantitative method still can't solve.
- It offers a concrete, technical reason for Slackr's decision to surface evidence rather than a derived score to instructors: even a purpose-built statistical model for estimating effort from commits acknowledges its output is "not a direct measurement but rather an inference," is sensitive to small-sample bias, and depends on an unresolved calibration parameter. Presenting raw signals (commit count, timing, last active) and letting a human interpret them avoids inheriting that model uncertainty as false precision.
- The Section 2.1 literature survey is a useful supplementary reading list if Slackr's research write-up wants more citations on contribution-measurement approaches beyond peer review and AI-assisted grading (its current focus), specifically for the GitHub/commit-based evidence side of the product.
