# Beyond Compliance: A Proposed Framework for Ethical Governance of Student Data in Learning Analytics

**Authors:** Sahana Varadaraju, Bharathwaj Vijayakumar
**Date:** Submitted to arXiv 4 August 2026. Accepted and published in the EDULEARN26 Proceedings (IATED, 2026); author manuscript with minor post-publication revisions.
**Source:** [arXiv:2608.03968](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03968) (PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.03968, HTML: https://arxiv.org/html/2608.03968v1). Related DOI: [10.21125/edulearn.2026.1927](https://doi.org/10.21125/edulearn.2026.1927)
**Relevance to Slackr:** Adjacent but directly useful. This is not a peer-assessment or contribution-scoring paper like the rest of the research folder. It is a governance framework for exactly the kind of institutional practice Slackr's own architecture performs: pulling observable student activity data (in Slackr's case, GitHub commits/PRs, Google Doc edits, Meet attendance) out of the tools students already use and turning it into something an instructor reviews. It gives Slackr a normative vocabulary and a checklist for the "is this ethical, not just legal" question that a report-generation tool built on student activity data will eventually be asked.

## Summary

The paper's starting observation is that learning analytics (LA) adoption in higher education has outpaced its governance. Institutions have built real capacity to monitor engagement, predict academic difficulty, and target support using student data, but the governance model for that capacity has stayed "compliance-first": centered on satisfying baseline legal floors like FERPA (US) and GDPR (EU) rather than on the harder normative questions of fairness, student agency, accountability, and educational purpose. The authors argue legal compliance is necessary but not sufficient, and propose a six-pillar model, LEAGUE, built through a conceptual synthesis of learning-analytics scholarship, educational data mining, data ethics, educational policy, value-sensitive design, and capability-oriented approaches to educational justice. The six pillars are: Lawfulness, Equity, Agency, Governance, Utility, and Ethics by Design. The authors demonstrate the framework's practical value through an illustrative "early alert" case study, showing how an institution could use LEAGUE to review an at-risk-student flagging system more transparently and meaningfully than a pure legal-compliance checklist would allow.

## Key Findings / Framework Components

- **The core critique:** legal compliance (FERPA/GDPR) is treated in current practice as the ceiling of governance rather than the floor. The paper's central claim is that "legal compliance is necessary, but it does not by itself resolve questions of fairness, student agency, accountability, or educational purpose."
- **Lawfulness:** the baseline legal layer (consent, data minimization, retention limits) that most institutional LA governance already covers, but treated here as only the first of six pillars, not the whole framework.
- **Equity:** whether the analytics system produces disparate impact across student subgroups, and whether the underlying data sources themselves are equally available/observable across those subgroups (a system built on data some students generate more visibly than others can encode inequity even with no biased intent).
- **Agency:** the degree of control and transparency students retain over data collected about them, including whether they can see, contest, or add context to what the system has inferred about them.
- **Governance:** institutional-level accountability structures, i.e. who is responsible for oversight, review, and course-correction when the analytics system is wrong or misused, as distinct from the individual student-facing Agency pillar.
- **Utility:** whether the analytics genuinely serves an educational purpose that benefits the student being analyzed, not just an institutional efficiency or risk-management goal.
- **Ethics by Design:** building the above considerations into the system architecture from the start, rather than retrofitting ethical review onto a system already in production.
- The paper is grounded in "value-sensitive design" and "capability-oriented approaches to educational justice," positioning student data governance as a design problem, not only a policy or legal-review problem.
- The early-alert case study is used to show LEAGUE catching things a compliance-only review would miss, e.g. a system that is FERPA/GDPR-legal but still opaque to the student it is about, or still unequally accurate/available across subgroups.

## Why This Matters for Slackr

- Slackr is, structurally, a learning-analytics system: it observes GitHub activity, Google Doc edits, and Meet attendance, and turns that into an instructor-facing report about individual students. This paper is the first item in the research folder that speaks to the governance of the *evidence pipeline itself*, rather than to the fairness of the *judgment* made from that evidence, which is what the rest of the folder (peer grading, AI contribution scoring, free-rider detection) addresses.
- The Equity pillar is a direct, concrete design risk for Slackr: if one team member does most of their real work in ways the connected tools don't observe well (e.g. offline design work, verbal coordination, pairing without commits under their own account), Slackr's evidence set will under-represent them relative to a teammate whose work happens to be GitHub-native. Slackr's README already anticipates this with its "Member context" feature (space for contribution the system can't observe), which is effectively an Agency-pillar mitigation for exactly this Equity risk, and this paper gives that design choice a citable theoretical grounding.
- The Agency pillar reinforces Slackr's "evidence, not verdict" positioning and its explicit "review suggested, not decided" framing: LEAGUE's argument that students should be able to see and contest what a system has inferred about them maps directly onto why Slackr surfaces evidence for instructor review rather than auto-generating a score or a grade.
- The Governance pillar is a natural extension point for Slackr's roadmap: the paper frames institutional accountability (who signs off on how the system is used, and what recourse exists if it's misused) as a distinct layer from student-facing transparency. Slackr's current design puts a human (the instructor) in the loop at the point of judgment, which satisfies part of this, but the paper suggests there's a further institutional-policy layer (data retention on connected accounts, scope of what's pulled, who can see it) worth documenting explicitly as the product matures.
- Combined with [[2025-11-ai-contribution-conflict-framework]] (only 1 of 11 surveyed contribution tools lets students add context) and [[2026-07-students-perceptions-peer-grading]] (anonymity and instructor oversight are the most effective mitigations against unfairness in peer-based assessment), this paper adds a third, complementary layer to Slackr's case: even when a tool sources contribution evidence from artifacts instead of peer ratings (avoiding the retaliation/collusion risks in the peer-grading literature), the artifact-based approach introduces its own governance obligations around equitable observability, student agency over inferred data, and institutional accountability, which LEAGUE gives Slackr a structured vocabulary to address proactively rather than reactively.
