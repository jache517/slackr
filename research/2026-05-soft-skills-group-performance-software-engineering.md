# Soft Skills and Group Performance in Higher Education: A Quantitative Study of Student Teams in Software Engineering

**Authors:** Kelian Schekkerman, Andrea Capiluppi (Faculty of Science and Engineering, University of Groningen, The Netherlands)
**Date:** Received 16 October 2025, accepted 29 April 2026, published online 9 May 2026. *Computer Applications in Engineering Education*, Vol. 34, Issue 3, article e70204.
**Source:** [DOI: 10.1002/cae.70204](https://doi.org/10.1002/cae.70204) (Wiley Online Library, open access, CC BY 4.0). Earlier conference version: "Soft Skills in Software Engineering Education: Improving Team Formation and Performance Prediction," *EASE 2025 Companion*, pp. 230-234 ([DOI: 10.1145/3727967.3756828](https://doi.org/10.1145/3727967.3756828)).
**Relevance to Slackr:** Directly on-topic and in Slackr's exact setting: real, project-based software engineering coursework, not a lab study or a K-12 classroom. It tracks the same kind of ongoing evidence Slackr collects (weekly status reports, self- and instructor-assessed skills, final deliverables) and ties it to two things Slackr's problem statement names explicitly: unequal contribution and fair workload distribution.

## Summary

The paper studies why some student software engineering teams perform well and others don't, using soft skills (communication, teamwork, leadership) and technical expertise as predictors, rather than treating group performance as something only visible at the final deliverable. The authors followed 118 students across 28 teams doing real, project-based coursework and built a dataset from three sources: self- and instructor-assessed skill ratings, weekly status reports submitted throughout the project, and final deliverable grades. They applied statistical correlation, significance testing, and clustering to find which factors predicted group success, and at what point in a project's lifecycle each factor mattered most.

The central finding is a shift over time in what drives performance. Early in a project, technical expertise, specifically the ability to extract and frame the core problem, was the strongest predictor of how well a team was doing. As projects progressed, that predictive power shifted to soft skills: communication and teamwork became the decisive factors in later phases. Teams with stronger soft skills did not just score higher on final deliverables; they also showed more balanced contributions across members, and teams whose members had compatible collaboration preferences showed fairer workload distribution. The authors frame this as evidence that group performance should be assessed and supported as a moving target across a project's timeline, not just measured once at the end.

## Key Findings

- **Technical skill predicts early performance; soft skills predict later performance.** "Technical expertise, particularly the ability to extract core problems, strongly influenced early project performance. In later phases, communication and teamwork emerged as decisive predictors of success" (abstract). This is a concrete, time-phased model of what "good teamwork" looks like at different points in a project, rather than a single static trait.
- **Soft skills correlate with balanced contribution, not just higher grades.** "Teams with stronger soft skills achieved more balanced contributions and higher deliverable grades" (abstract): the study ties a soft-skills measure directly to contribution balance, giving an evidentiary link between team communication quality and the kind of unequal-contribution problem Slackr is built around.
- **Collaboration preferences predict fairer workload distribution.** "Collaboration preferences were associated with fairer workload distribution" (abstract), suggesting that how a team is composed and how its members prefer to work together has a measurable effect on whether work ends up spread unevenly, independent of any single member's ability or effort.
- **The evidence base is longitudinal and multi-source, not a single end-of-project survey.** The dataset combined self-assessment, instructor assessment, weekly status reports, and final deliverables, then used clustering and significance testing across that combined signal rather than relying on one instrument. This mirrors Slackr's own design choice to pull from multiple observable sources (commits, docs, meetings) rather than a single self-report.
- **The stated goal is curriculum and team-formation design, not scoring individuals.** The paper's own framing is that "integrating structured soft skills assessment and strategic team formation into software engineering curricula enhances team performance and reduces dysfunctional dynamics," and that the work aims to align "education with industry expectations and preparing graduates for collaborative professional contexts" (abstract) — an instructional/organizational intervention, not an automated grading or free-rider-flagging system.

## Why This Matters for Slackr

- This is the closest paper in the folder to Slackr's actual student population and setting: real software engineering teams doing real project-based coursework, tracked over the life of the project rather than assessed once at the end. It strengthens the "why this problem, why now" background section with a 2026, peer-reviewed, open-access citation grounded in the exact context (software engineering coursework, team contribution imbalance, workload fairness) Slackr targets.
- The early-technical / late-soft-skills timing finding is a useful design signal for when Slackr's evidence collection matters most. If communication and teamwork become the decisive factor for group success as a project matures, that argues for Slackr's activity evidence (commits, doc edits, meeting attendance) being reviewed at multiple checkpoints across a project rather than only once at the end, since the same team could look fine on technical output early on while a collaboration problem is only becoming visible later.
- The direct link between soft-skills measures and "balanced contributions" gives Slackr an external empirical anchor for its core premise, that contribution imbalance is a real, measurable phenomenon connected to team dynamics, not merely a perception problem. That helps justify collecting objective activity evidence as a complement to the kind of self-/instructor-assessment this study relies on.
- Its multi-source, longitudinal dataset design (self-assessment, instructor assessment, weekly reports, final grades) is a structural precedent for Slackr's own evidence-not-verdict design: sensible team-level judgments in this literature already come from combining several observable signals over time, not from any single score, which supports the project's explicit choice to avoid producing one automated contribution number.
- The paper's own framing, that the intervention is meant to inform curriculum design, team formation, and instructor support rather than to auto-grade individuals, lines up closely with Slackr's "evidence, not verdict" positioning and can be cited alongside [[2026-05-stop-automating-peer-review]] and [[2025-11-ai-contribution-conflict-framework]] as part of the folder's running argument that the credible research direction here is decision support for instructors, not automated scoring.
