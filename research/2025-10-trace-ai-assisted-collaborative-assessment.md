# TRACE: AI-Assisted Assessment of Collaborative Projects in Computer Science Education

**Authors:** Songmei Yu (Felician University), Andrew Zagula (Bridgewater-Raritan High School)
**Date:** arXiv preprint, October 2025 (arXiv ID 2510.03998 indicates an October 2025 submission window; a later revision appears to have been indexed in early 2026)
**Source:** [arXiv:2510.03998](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.03998) (HTML: https://arxiv.org/html/2510.03998)
**Relevance to Slackr:** Directly on-topic, and the closest existing system to Slackr found in this research pass. TRACE is a real, deployed tool that mines GitHub repository activity and communication logs to assess individual contribution in student software projects, exactly Slackr's own approach, but it takes the opposite stance on the final step: TRACE computes an automated numeric grade, where Slackr deliberately stops at evidence for instructor review.

## Summary

TRACE is a semi-automated framework built to solve the same problem Slackr targets: instructors grading group software projects lack visibility into who actually did the work, and both equal-grade distribution and subjective peer evaluation are unreliable at scale. TRACE has three modules: a Project Quality Assessment Module (PQAM) that scores the group's overall deliverable (code quality via static analysis, test coverage, documentation via NLP, functionality via automated test execution, usability via heuristic/accessibility checks), an Individual Contribution Analyzer (ICA) that scores each student's personal contribution (commit analysis, code ownership via `git blame`, issue tracker activity, code review participation), and a Grading Engine (GE) that combines the two into a single final grade per student, flagging statistical outliers for manual instructor review. It was piloted in a real Spring 2025 software engineering course (20 students, 5 teams, 8 weeks, GitHub Classroom).

## Key Findings

- **Pilot results were strong on every metric TRACE measured.** AI-generated grades correlated with instructor-assigned grades at Pearson r = 0.91. Students rated perceived fairness 4.3/5 and transparency 4.5/5 on a post-project survey. Instructor grading time dropped 45% versus manual rubric-based grading in prior semesters. About 12% of submissions needed manual intervention, typically due to flagged anomalies or non-code contributions the system couldn't see.
- **The commit-gaming problem is real and TRACE built a specific defense against it.** One pilot student "attempted to inflate activity by making frequent whitespace-only and comment-only commits." The system's diff-analysis layer downweighted these and flagged the submission, "preventing artificial grade inflation." This is direct field evidence that naive commit-counting is gameable and that diff-level content analysis, not just commit frequency, is needed to make repository evidence trustworthy.
- **Uneven contribution is detectable and instructors act on it.** In one case a single team member authored 58% of the production code and resolved most issues; the system raised that student's individual score, and "the instructor subsequently confirmed it." In a case with even distribution (within ±5%), the AI-generated grades matched closely with no override needed.
- **TRACE explicitly names what its own signals miss.** The paper's own limitations section states: "Certain contributions, such as design ideation, presentation preparation, and team facilitation, are difficult to quantify automatically. Students who used GitHub or issue trackers inconsistently may have had contributions that were underrepresented in the collected data. In addition, students with weaker English proficiency or non-native grammar patterns may receive lower documentation scores."
- **Peer evaluation tools (CATME, SPARKPLUS) are cited as the status quo and criticized on the same grounds as this folder's other sources**: "these systems are susceptible to bias, collusion, and peer pressure ... peer reviews may fail to accurately reflect technical contributions, particularly in large or distributed teams."
- **The paper treats "AI replaces the instructor" as out of scope by design.** Repeated framing throughout: TRACE is meant to "augment instructor judgment rather than replace it," preserving "instructor oversight and judgment" and logging "all grading decisions and manual overrides ... to support transparency during grade reviews or disputes."
- **Ethical section flags the exact risks that matter for any system like Slackr**: transparency (avoiding "black-box" grading via interpretable dashboards), fairness (periodic statistical audits and anonymized manual review to catch bias against non-native-language or atypical-collaboration students), privacy/data governance (FERPA/GDPR compliance, restricted access, encrypted storage, informed consent), and human oversight (structured appeal mechanisms).
- **Listed future work includes "Enhanced Peer Review,"** which the authors frame as combining peer scores with ICA's behavioral signals so that "reviewer reliability can also be estimated from historical agreement with observed contribution patterns," i.e. weighting peer opinion by how well it matches observed evidence, rather than trusting it outright.

## Why This Matters for Slackr

TRACE is the single closest prior system to Slackr found across this research effort. It validates, with a real classroom deployment and quantified numbers, that Slackr's core evidence sources (commit history, code ownership, issue tracker activity, and by extension meeting/document activity) are viable, instructor-trusted signals: r = 0.91 alignment with instructor judgment and a 45% cut in grading time are strong existence proof that automatically collected artifact evidence is not just plausible in theory but works in practice.

At the same time, TRACE is the most direct point of contrast for Slackr's central design decision. TRACE goes one step further than Slackr by design: it collapses PQS and NCS into a single automated `Score = PQS*0.6 + NCS*0.4` and produces a grade, only routing outliers to a human. Slackr's README explicitly scopes out "contribution scoring, automatic grading, free-rider classification" and stops at organizing evidence for the instructor. TRACE's own admitted failure modes make a strong case for why that line matters: it under-scores design ideation, facilitation, and inconsistent tool use, and its NLP-based documentation scoring risks penalizing non-native English writers, exactly the kind of invisible, context-dependent contribution that Slackr's "member context" step (space for contribution the system can't observe) is built to capture rather than silently miss inside a formula.

TRACE's whitespace-commit gaming case is a concrete, real-world instance of the same "evidence can still be gamed" risk flagged more theoretically in [[2026-05-stop-automating-peer-review]] and in the strategyproofing literature in [[2026-05-peerbts-strategyproof-peer-selection]]; it confirms Slackr will eventually need some diff-level or content-level filtering (not just raw commit/comment counts) if it wants its GitHub evidence to be trustworthy rather than superficially countable. TRACE's proposed "Enhanced Peer Review" future direction, weighting peer feedback by its historical agreement with observed activity, is also a useful design idea Slackr could borrow if it ever incorporates any peer input alongside its artifact evidence.

Combined with [[2025-11-ai-contribution-conflict-framework]] (11-tool survey showing almost no existing system lets students add explanatory context) and [[2026-07-students-perceptions-peer-grading]] (the social/emotional cost of peer-assigned scores), TRACE completes the picture from the opposite angle: it shows that once you build the automated evidence pipeline, the temptation is to go all the way to a grade, and this paper's own limitations section is the best available argument for why Slackr should resist that and keep the human instructor as the final judgment layer.
