# Leveraging Peer, Self, and Teacher Assessments for Generative AI-Enhanced Feedback

**Authors:** Alvaro Becerra, Ruth Cobos (Universidad Autonoma de Madrid, Department of Computer Science Engineering)
**Date:** arXiv preprint, submitted December 2025
**Source:** [arXiv:2512.18306](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.18306) (PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.18306)
**Relevance to Slackr:** Directly on-topic and a new angle not yet covered in this folder. This is an empirical study of a real, deployed system (AICoFe) that combines teacher, peer, and self-assessment of student work, quantifies how much each source agrees with (and diverges from) the others, and uses that evidence to redesign its AI feedback layer around weighted aggregation and explicit bias detection rather than treating every evaluator as equally reliable. It is the first paper in this folder to give hard numbers on how self-assessment specifically compares to peer and teacher judgment, and the first to propose a concrete mechanism (weighting plus bias flags) for combining multiple imperfect human signals into one AI output.

## Summary

AICoFe ("AI-based Collaborative Feedback system") is a deployed classroom tool at Universidad Autonoma de Madrid that has students give and receive structured feedback on oral presentations using a validated 15-item rubric (Content, Design, Delivery; five-point Likert scale per item). For each presentation, three sources score the same student against the same rubric: the teacher, two randomly assigned peers, and the presenter themselves (self-assessment, done after watching a video recording of their own talk). The teacher additionally assigns an independent global grade not derived from the rubric. The study analyzes 46 full evaluation sets (46 undergraduate engineering students, one presentation each) using inter-rater agreement statistics (weighted Cohen's kappa, Gwet's AC2, Kendall's W), correlation analysis (Pearson, Spearman) between each source and the teacher's overall grade, and paired-sample tests for systematic bias between evaluator groups. The findings are then used to redesign AICoFe's generative-AI feedback layer, GePeTo, from a system that simply averages teacher and peer scores into one that explicitly weights sources by reliability and flags divergence between them before generating feedback.

## Key Findings

- **Peer and self-assessment both track the teacher's judgment, but with a real gap between them.** Peer rubric scores correlated with the teacher's overall grade at r=0.69 (Spearman rho=0.68); self-assessment correlated only at r=0.59 (rho=0.67). Both were statistically significant (p<0.001), but self-assessment was consistently the weakest predictor of teacher judgment of any source measured.
- **Overall agreement across all three sources was moderate, not high.** Kendall's W (global concordance) was 0.51 for teacher+peers and dropped to 0.46 once self-assessment was included. Gwet's AC2 (a more robust agreement statistic than Cohen's kappa for skewed rating distributions) held steadier at 0.66 for both groupings, and the paper explicitly flags the "kappa paradox": raw Cohen's kappa values (0.22 to 0.30) looked far weaker than the underlying agreement actually was, because of how kappa handles skewed score distributions. This is a methodological warning for anyone using raw kappa alone to judge whether peer/self ratings are "reliable enough."
- **There is a real, statistically significant scoring bias, but it is inflation, not favoritism.** Students (both self- and peer-raters) scored about half a point to a third of a point higher on average than the teacher's rubric scores (teacher vs. peers: -0.49, p=0.008; self vs. teacher rubric: +0.36, p=0.037). Critically, peer scores did not differ significantly from self-assessment scores, which the authors read as evidence that the bias is a general positive/leniency bias shared across student raters, not reciprocity or friendship-based favoritism between specific peer pairs, consistent with prior literature they cite showing reciprocity effects are typically negligible.
- **The teacher was internally stricter on the rubric than on her own holistic grade.** The teacher's own rubric-based mean score was significantly lower than her own independently-assigned overall grade (-0.37, p<0.001) even though both came from the same person. The paper interprets this as detailed, itemized rubrics producing stricter scoring than a single holistic judgment call, a useful data point for anyone designing structured evidence forms that will later be compared against a human's gut-level assessment.
- **The system's actual design response was to stop averaging and start weighting.** Because teacher, peer, and self-assessment showed measurably different reliability and different systematic bias, the authors propose (and describe as their concrete contribution) redesigning AICoFe's AI feedback generator to: (1) weight evaluator inputs unequally, giving more influence to the source shown to correlate most strongly with the teacher's grade; (2) run a bias/divergence detection step during preprocessing that explicitly looks for patterns like self-assessment overestimation or peer leniency; and (3) have the generative model reason about and surface those divergences in its output, rather than silently blending scores.

## Notable Quotes

> "A current limitation of AICoFe and similar AI-enhanced feedback systems lies in their equal weighting of evaluative sources, typically treating teacher and peer inputs as equally reliable, without considering potential variations in bias, consistency, or perspective among them."

> "The higher mean scores in peer and self-assessments reflect a general positive scoring bias, where raters systematically overestimate performance... the absence of strong systematic deviations between peer and self-assessment scores... suggests that bias was more related to overall score inflation than reciprocal favoritism."

> "This uniform weighting assumes equivalent reliability among evaluators, overlooking systematic divergences identified in our analyses, for example, that teachers apply stricter criteria while peers tend to provide more lenient ratings. Such discrepancies highlight the need for a more nuanced integration mechanism that accounts for evaluator bias and relative expertise."

> "In this proposed design, GePeTo evolves from a purely data-driven aggregator into a context-aware feedback mediator capable of integrating human evaluations through pedagogically informed weighting and interpretive bias awareness."

## Why This Matters for Slackr

- This is empirical, quantified support for treating peer-reported input as one signal among several rather than ground truth on its own: even under close to ideal conditions (a validated rubric, anonymized peer assignment, training on the rubric beforehand), peer ratings still only correlated at r=0.69 with the teacher's independent judgment, and self-assessment fell further to r=0.59. If Slackr ever ingests self-reported or peer-reported context alongside artifact evidence (commits, PRs, meeting attendance), this paper gives a concrete, citable expectation for how much that input should be trusted relative to a more evidence-grounded signal, and argues for weighting rather than flat averaging.
- The inflation-not-favoritism finding is a useful nuance on top of the retaliation/collusion findings in [[2026-07-students-perceptions-peer-grading]]: self- and peer-reported signals may drift upward as a general leniency effect even when there is no targeted bias between specific individuals. That is a distinct failure mode from the fear-of-conflict and retaliation risks already documented, and one Slackr's artifact-based evidence approach (commits, PRs, doc edits) is structurally immune to in a way that any self-report field would not be.
- The paper's concrete design response, weighting inputs by demonstrated reliability and explicitly detecting divergence between sources before generating output, is a directly reusable pattern if Slackr's pipeline ever needs to reconcile automated artifact evidence with student-submitted context or instructor notes. Rather than blending everything into one score, the AICoFe redesign argues for surfacing where sources disagree, which lines up with Slackr's own "evidence, not verdict" stance: divergence between sources is itself information for the instructor, not noise to be averaged away.
- The kappa-paradox methodological note is worth flagging for Slackr's own future validation work: if Slackr is ever evaluated by comparing its evidence summaries or flags against instructor judgment, raw agreement statistics like Cohen's kappa can understate real agreement when score distributions are skewed (as contribution and grading distributions typically are), so a more robust statistic like Gwet's AC2 should be preferred when reporting how well the tool's output matches instructor judgment.
- Together with [[2026-07-students-perceptions-peer-grading]] (social/emotional cost of peer grading), [[2026-05-stop-automating-peer-review]] (risks of full automation), and [[2025-11-ai-contribution-conflict-framework]] (survey of existing contribution tools), this paper adds the missing empirical piece on self-assessment specifically, and is the first source in this folder to propose a concrete, evidence-driven mechanism for combining multiple imperfect human signals rather than simply picking one (peer ratings) or discarding all of them in favor of pure automation.
