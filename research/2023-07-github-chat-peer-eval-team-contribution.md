# Combining GitHub, Chat, and Peer Evaluation Data to Assess Individual Contributions to Team Software Development Projects

**Authors:** Christopher Hundhausen (Oregon State University), Phill Conrad (UC Santa Barbara), Olusola Adesope (Washington State University), Ahsun Tariq (Oregon State University)
**Date:** Published July 2023
**Source:** ACM Transactions on Computing Education, Vol. 23, No. 3, Article 33 - [doi.org/10.1145/3593592](https://doi.org/10.1145/3593592) (also mirrored at https://pconrad.github.io/files/paper034.pdf)
**Relevance to Slackr:** Directly on-topic and empirically load-bearing. This is the closest thing found so far to a direct validation study of Slackr's core premise: that observable digital-trace evidence (GitHub activity plus chat activity) meaningfully tracks how teammates actually perceive each other's contributions. It has not previously been added to this research folder.

## Summary

The authors ran a correlational study across 23 undergraduate software teams (n = 117 students) at two North American universities (UC Santa Barbara and Washington State University), across four course offerings. For every student they collected four parallel data streams: GitHub activity (commits, lines changed, completed "stories"/issues), team chat activity (Slack or Microsoft Teams: message count, word count, character count), CATME peer evaluation ratings (a validated survey used widely in higher ed, scored on Contribution and Interaction scales), and individual course grades. They then ran Pearson correlations across all of these metrics, and separately used the Gini Index (borrowed from economics, used elsewhere to measure open-source contribution inequality) to test whether unequal contribution within a team predicts worse peer evaluation outcomes. This paper is an extension of two earlier studies by an overlapping author group; this version adds the chat-data analysis and the team-level equality analysis on top of prior individual-only GitHub analyses.

## Key Findings

- Of 36 possible correlations between GitHub metrics and chat metrics, 33 (92%) were statistically significant, and 29 (81%) were at least moderate in strength (Pearson R). Students who code more also chat more about the project.
- Of 36 possible correlations between the objective metrics (GitHub + chat) and the subjective metrics (peer evaluation ratings + individual grades), 30 (83%) were statistically significant, 23 (64%) at moderate strength or higher.
- Chat contribution volume (messages, words, characters) was positively correlated with CATME peer-rated Contribution (R around 0.36-0.45) and Interaction (R around 0.34-0.38) scores, not just with GitHub activity. This extends prior work that had only shown the GitHub-side correlation.
- At the team level, more equal distribution of commits and completed stories (lower Gini Index) was associated with higher average peer evaluation ratings (R = -0.45 for commit equality vs. contribution rating) and lower variance in those ratings across teammates (R = 0.58-0.67 for the corresponding inequality-vs-variance relationships). In plain terms: teams where code work was split unevenly tended to rate each other lower and less consistently.
- Notably, this equality effect held for GitHub metrics (commits, stories) but did not hold for chat metrics: equal chat participation was not correlated with peer eval outcomes. The authors' working explanation is that they could only capture the one "official" sanctioned chat channel, and students likely also communicated through unmeasured channels (Discord, texts, DMs, calls), so true chat-equality patterns may have been invisible to the study.
- The authors explicitly invoke Buffardi's prior hypothesis of a "halo effect" (i.e. that peer ratings might just reflect general likability/reputation rather than real contribution) and argue their correlational results are evidence against that: contribution and communication activity, not just reputation, appears to drive peer ratings.
- They flag Campbell's law as a real risk: once students know objective metrics (commit counts, chat volume) feed into assessment, some may game those metrics rather than genuinely contribute more.
- Correlations with individual course grades were present but weaker (R = 0.20-0.30) than the GitHub/chat-to-peer-eval correlations, suggesting the digital-trace metrics track peer-perceived teamwork specifically, more than general academic performance.

## Why This Matters for Slackr

- This is the strongest available empirical grounding for Slackr's foundational bet: that artifacts already produced by the tools a team uses (GitHub, chat) are not just a convenient proxy but are measurably correlated with how teammates actually judge each other's contribution. Slackr's README leans on this assumption implicitly; this paper is a citable source for it.
- The equality/Gini finding directly supports a specific, buildable feature idea for Slackr: flagging skewed commit/issue distribution within a team as an "early warning sign," which is literally the paper's own stated implication (Section 6.1.3, "Equality Metrics as Early Warning Signs of Trouble"). This is a concrete, low-risk instantiation of "evidence, not verdict": surfacing an inequality signal to the instructor without asserting who is at fault.
- The negative result on chat-equality (no correlation with peer ratings) is a useful caution for Slackr's Google Meet/communication-evidence ambitions: a single official channel's activity counts are an incomplete signal of participation, and Slackr should be careful not to overweight one communication source (e.g. Meet attendance alone) as a stand-in for "engagement," echoing the same channel-coverage gap this paper explicitly names as its own limitation.
- The Campbell's-law warning is directly relevant to Slackr's explicit design choice to avoid outputting a contribution score: if Slackr ever moved toward a number instead of organized evidence, this paper is a citable reason that doing so invites gaming (commit-padding, trivial PRs, filler chat messages) rather than genuine collaboration.
- This paper complements [[2024-10-contribution-rate-imputation-theory]] and [[2025-01-teamwork-dynamics-gitlab-discrepancy]] (which look at inferring/reconciling contribution from repo data) by providing the peer-perception side of the same coin: it shows that repo-and-chat-derived signals track what teammates themselves would say about each other, which is exactly the bridge Slackr's "evidence, not verdict" model is trying to build between automatic collection and human judgment.
- Methodologically, it is also a caution: this is a correlational, pandemic-era, small-N (23 teams) study run in only two courses, and the authors are explicit that generalization beyond short, small academic teams is unproven. Any citation of its findings in Slackr's own materials should keep that caveat attached.
