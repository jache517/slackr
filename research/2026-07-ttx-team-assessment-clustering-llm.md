# Assessment in Team Problem-Solving Exercises in Computing Education

**Authors:** Valdemar Švábenský, Jan Vykopal, Sukrit Leelaluk, Pavel Čeleda, Fumiya Okubo, Atsushi Shimada
**Date:** Submitted to arXiv 21 July 2026. Full paper accepted for the main track of the 56th IEEE Frontiers in Education Conference (FIE 2026), Paphos, Cyprus.
**Source:** [arXiv:2607.19209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.19209) (PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.19209, HTML: https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19209v1)
**Relevance to Slackr:** Directly on-topic. This is a computing-education paper that validates automated, data-driven team assessment (clustering and LLM scoring over logged team activity) against instructor-assigned rubric scores, and finds one method valid/reliable/cheap and the other highly model-dependent. It gives Slackr a close empirical precedent for its own core bet: that logged activity (in Slackr's case, GitHub/Docs/Meet; in this paper's case, tabletop-exercise action and chat logs) can stand in for, or accelerate, instructor judgment, plus a concrete cautionary data point about which automated method (clustering vs. LLM-as-judge) is more trustworthy for that job.

## Summary

The paper addresses assessment of student teams in tabletop exercises (TTXs): structured, open-ended group exercises (here, cybersecurity incident-response simulations) where teams work through a scenario together and are typically assessed by an instructor against a standardized rubric after the fact. The authors' motivating problem is one Slackr will recognize: TTX learning platforms already record everything a team does, actions taken and messages exchanged, but that logged data is not being turned into timely, scalable assessment. Instructors doing this by hand produce delayed, inconsistent, and non-scalable feedback.

The authors built an original dataset from 81 participants across two countries using INJECT, an open-source TTX learning platform, and tested two automated approaches to turn the logged team data into an assessment: (1) clustering teams by how they approached the exercise, and (2) prompting large language models to score team communication against the same standardized rubric instructors use. Both methods were evaluated against ground-truth instructor scores. Clustering was found valid, reliable, and computationally cheap; it does not produce a score per se but groups teams that approached the task similarly, letting an instructor give one round of targeted feedback to an entire cluster rather than grading every team from scratch. The LLM approach was far more model-dependent: GPT-4o frequently disagreed with instructor scores, while GPT-5.2 (evaluated in the same pipeline) showed considerably lower error against the instructor ground truth. The resulting methods have been integrated into INJECT itself, and the authors have released the dataset, software tools, and a full TTX scenario publicly to support community adoption.

## Key Findings

- **Two competing automated-assessment strategies, one data source.** Both clustering and LLM scoring were run over the same underlying logged team data (actions taken in the exercise, plus team communication), and evaluated against the same ground truth (instructor scores on a standardized rubric), letting the paper compare the two approaches head-to-head rather than validating either in isolation.
- **Clustering: valid, reliable, and cheap, but coarser.** Grouping teams by how similarly they approached the TTX let instructors deliver one round of "targeted feedback per cluster" instead of one round of feedback per team, and the method held up as valid and reliable with low computational cost. It does not directly output a score comparable to a rubric grade, its value is in triage and pattern-finding, not final assessment.
- **LLM-as-judge: powerful but highly sensitive to model choice.** Using an LLM to score team communication directly against the instructor's rubric showed a large swing in accuracy purely from model version: GPT-4o "frequently disagreed with instructor scores," while GPT-5.2 "demonstrated considerably lower error" on the identical task and rubric. The paper treats this model-to-model gap as a central finding, not a footnote, underscoring that "LLM-based assessment" is not one fixed capability but one that shifts materially with each new model generation.
- **Real deployment, not just a lab result.** The methods were not left as a standalone research artifact; they were integrated back into INJECT, the actual platform instructors use to run TTXs, explicitly framed around "scalability and teaching practice." The authors also released the dataset, software tools, and a full TTX scenario publicly, positioning the work for community reuse and replication rather than a one-off case study.
- **The underlying gap the paper targets is structural, not just a tooling gap.** The authors frame the TTX assessment problem as one where "the complex, open-ended nature of TTXs often leads to delayed or incomplete feedback" even though the platform is already capturing everything needed to assess a team, the bottleneck is turning captured activity into judgment at the speed and scale instructors need, which is the same bottleneck Slackr targets on the GitHub/Docs/Meet side of group coursework.

## Why This Matters for Slackr

- This is the closest methodological precedent in the folder to Slackr's actual mechanism: mining already-logged team activity (actions plus communication, in this paper's case; commits, doc edits, and meeting attendance in Slackr's) and using it to produce something an instructor can act on faster than manual review, with instructor judgment kept as the validation ground truth rather than being replaced by it. Like [[2025-10-trace-ai-assisted-collaborative-assessment]] and [[2025-05-llm-contribution-summarization-software-projects]], this adds another concrete existence proof that log-mining-for-assessment is an active, credible computing-education research direction in 2026, not a novel or unproven approach specific to Slackr.
- The clustering-versus-LLM comparison is a useful design signal if Slackr ever needs to summarize or group similar contribution patterns across a roster of students (e.g., flagging "these three students show a similar commit/edit shape") rather than only scoring individuals: the paper's finding that clustering was valid, reliable, and cheap while LLM scoring was volatile across model versions suggests Slackr should treat any LLM-generated summary or score in its pipeline as the less stable component, worth validating per model version rather than assuming any one calibration will hold as models change.
- The GPT-4o vs. GPT-5.2 accuracy gap is a direct, citable caution for Slackr's own use of LLMs to summarize evidence or draft narrative feedback: rubric-aligned accuracy is not a stable property of "using an LLM," it is a property of a specific model at a specific point in time, and jumped substantially between two model generations on an identical task in this study. Any Slackr feature that uses an LLM to characterize a student's contribution should be periodically re-validated against instructor judgment rather than assumed stable across model upgrades.
- The paper's framing that assessment platforms already capture the data needed for feedback but fail to convert it into timely judgment is close to a mission statement for Slackr's own value proposition on the software-team side, and is a strong, recent (July 2026), peer-venue-accepted citation for the project's "why this problem, why now" background section.
- Combined with [[2026-05-peerbts-strategyproof-peer-selection]] and [[2026-05-stop-automating-peer-review]], this paper adds a third angle to the folder's growing "how do you validate an automated assessment system" thread: PeerBTS addresses the incentive structure of peer input, Stop Automating Peer Review addresses evaluation rigor before deployment, and this paper adds a concrete worked example of validating two different automation strategies against instructor ground truth in a live, currently-deployed classroom tool, which is the closest thing in the folder to a template for how Slackr itself might eventually validate its own contribution reports against instructor judgment.
