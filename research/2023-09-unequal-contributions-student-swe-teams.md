# The Impact of Unequal Contributions in Student Software Engineering Team Projects

**Authors:** Kamilla Kopec-Harding, Sukru Eraslan, Bowen Cai, Suzanne M. Embury, Caroline Jay (University of Manchester and Middle East Technical University, Northern Cyprus Campus)
**Date:** Preprint submitted 5 September 2023, in press at *The Journal of Systems and Software*
**Source:** [Manchester Research Explorer record](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/the-impact-of-unequal-contributions-in-student-software-engineeri/) / [author accepted manuscript PDF](https://pure.manchester.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/275423912/The_Impact_of_Unequal_Contributions_in_Student_Software_Engineering_Team_Projects_JSS.pdf)
**Relevance to Slackr:** Directly on-topic and empirically load-bearing. This is a large, multi-cohort, quantitative study of exactly the phenomenon Slackr is built around: unequal contribution in student software engineering team projects, measured from the same kind of Git/repository data Slackr collects. It gives Slackr's problem statement hard numbers, and its stated construct-validity limitation (Git data alone misses non-coding contribution) is effectively a peer-reviewed argument for Slackr's multi-source, evidence-plus-context design.

## Summary

The authors mined Git repository data from three consecutive cohorts (2017/18, 2018/19, 2019/20) of second-year software engineering students at the University of Manchester, covering four separate team assignments per cohort (three short exercises in a module called SE1, plus one longer 10-week project in a module called SE2). This produced individual-level contribution data for roughly 700-750 students across well over 100 teams per module, combined with team grades and SonarQube code-quality metrics for the same projects. The paper is framed around five research questions: how unequal is contribution within teams, does an individual's over/under-contribution persist into their next team project, does experiencing inequality on one project affect a student's contribution on the next, does inequality relate to team grade or code quality, and does the git-derived measure of inequality actually predict when a team perceives its contribution as unfair (operationalised as a successful grade-redistribution complaint to the course team).

## Key Findings / Data

- **No equal team was ever observed.** Across all three cohorts and both modules, not a single team had every member contributing at least 90% of their fair share of lines of code. The paper states this plainly: "Equal teams where every team member contributed at least 90% of their fair share were not found in our cohorts."
- **Most individuals under-contribute.** Across assignments and cohorts, 51.4%-59.7% of students were classified as under-contributors (< 90% of fair share by lines of code), while only 5.5%-13.7% were "adequate" contributors (90-110% of fair share).
- **Severe imbalance is common, not rare.** The proportion of students contributing less than 25% of their fair share ranged from 8.16% to 26.5% depending on assignment/cohort, and "one-person teams" (one member did at least twice the code of the second-highest contributor) affected 16.2%-40.5% of teams in the short exercises and 20%-24.3% of teams in the long project.
- **Team-level inequality (Gini coefficient) is consistently in the "low equality" to "extreme inequality" band.** Median Gini coefficients across assignments ranged from 0.375 to 0.56, all above the 0.3 threshold the authors define as "low equality," with several assignment/cohort combinations exceeding 0.4 ("extreme inequality"). Their summary conclusion: "in all assignments, the majority of teams had 'low equality of contribution' (Gini 0.3-0.4) or 'extreme inequality of contribution' (Gini > 0.4)."
- **Contribution style is situational, not a fixed trait.** Between 41% and 94.7% of students (depending on category) changed contribution category (under/adequate/over) between one project and the next; free-riders in one project were very unlikely to be free-riders again in the next (12.5%-20% repeat rate). The authors conclude: "when a student under contributes to one project, it does not necessarily mean that the student will under contribute to another project."
- **Inequality did not reliably predict worse grades or worse code quality.** "Teams affected by free-riding and over-contribution did not achieve lower grades or produce lower quality source code," and the few significant associations found were not consistent across cohorts.
- **A precise threshold for when teams actually complain.** Using ROC analysis, the strongest predictors of a team successfully escalating a contribution complaint were the Gini coefficient (optimal cut point 0.40) and the smallest individual's relative contribution (optimal cut point 14% of fair share). In their words: "Team-perceived inequality was more likely (i) when the contribution of the least active team-member was less than 14% of their fair-share, or (ii) when the Gini coefficient approached a value indicating extreme inequality (> 0.4)."
- **Students strongly tolerate low contribution and rarely report it.** The 14% threshold is very low, and the paper explicitly links this to reluctance to report: "This high tolerance for low contribution is consistent with prior work which found that students are reluctant to report social loafers to the course team apart from serious cases."
- **Explicit limitation: Git data alone misses real contribution.** In the threats-to-validity section, the authors state their metric "does not take account of non-coding contributions such as meeting attendance, participation in team communications, design, documentation, etc.," and that these should also be tracked via "attendance of the meetings, the people who actively participate in team communications, the people who work on design and documentation, and how long they work on these tasks."

## Why This Matters for Slackr

- The 14%-fair-share / Gini-0.4 findings give Slackr's problem statement a citable, quantified answer to "how bad is unequal contribution, really, and when do students actually complain?" Slackr's README opens with the same causal chain the paper's data supports: students know contribution is unequal, but rarely raise it until it is severe.
- The finding that inequality does **not** reliably show up in worse grades or worse code quality is important ammunition against building a scoring or auto-flagging system: if the artifact evidence (Git activity) doesn't cleanly predict outcomes instructors already grade on, that reinforces Slackr's "evidence, not verdict" stance over an automated free-rider classifier or contribution score.
- The construct-validity limitation the authors flag themselves, that git-only metrics miss meeting attendance, communication, design and documentation work, is close to a direct citation for Slackr's multi-source design (GitHub + Google Docs + Google Meet) and its "Member context" feature (space for contribution the system can't observe). This paper is the strongest empirical grounding found so far in the folder for *why* a single-source (commits-only) contribution tool would be misleading, and by extension for Slackr's decision to pull from multiple observable sources rather than just Git.
- The situational, non-persistent nature of contribution style (a free-rider on one project is very unlikely to be one again) is a useful caution against any feature that would carry a "reputation" or historical flag for a student across projects; each project's evidence should be assessed on its own, which matches Slackr's per-project report structure.
- Recommendation 3 in the paper, "consider providing alternatives to 'reporting' team members," because teams are reluctant to formally report under-contributors, is close to a direct academic endorsement of Slackr's core mechanism: an automatically generated, evidence-based report removes the burden of a student having to personally initiate an accusation against a teammate.
- This paper complements [[2025-11-ai-contribution-conflict-framework]] (which surveys 11 peer/contribution tools and found only 1 lets students add context) and [[2026-08-league-ethical-governance-student-data]] (on equitable observability of the underlying evidence pipeline): together the three make a layered case for Slackr's design, empirical scale of the problem here, tool landscape gap there, and governance framing in the third.
