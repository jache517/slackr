# Enabling and Inhibitory Pathways of University Students' Willingness to Disclose AI Use: A Cognition-Affect-Conation Perspective

**Authors:** Yiran Du (University of Cambridge), Huimin He (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University)
**Date:** Submitted to arXiv 23 April 2026 (v1)
**Source:** [arXiv:2604.21733](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.21733) (HTML: https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21733, PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.21733)
**Relevance to Slackr:** High, and structurally different from the rest of the research folder. Every other paper here studies contribution-evidence collection or peer/AI-based scoring. This one studies the psychology of *voluntary self-disclosure* itself: why a student would or wouldn't tell an instructor the truth about how a piece of work got made. That is exactly the mechanism Slackr's "Member context" self-report feature depends on, and it gives quantitative, not just anecdotal, backing to Track 1's core reframe away from accusatory framing.

## Summary

The study asks why university students choose to disclose (or hide) their use of AI tools on academic work, using a Cognition-Affect-Conation (CAC) framework: cognitive perceptions of the environment shape affective/emotional states, which shape behavioural intention. The authors ran a sequential explanatory mixed-methods study: a 546-student survey (China, multi-university convenience sample, 53.8% female / 46.2% male, 77.1% undergraduate) analyzed with structural equation modelling (SEM), followed by 22 semi-structured interviews with students purposively sampled from the top and bottom quartiles of disclosure intention, coded to thematic saturation (inter-coder κ = 0.87).

The model has two pathways into "willingness to disclose AI use": an **enabling pathway** running through psychological safety, and an **inhibitory pathway** running through evaluation apprehension.

## Key Findings / Data

Quantitative (SEM path coefficients, all significant):

- Psychological safety → willingness to disclose: **β = 0.48, p < .001**
- Perceived fairness → psychological safety: β = 0.29, p < .001
- Perceived teacher support → psychological safety: β = 0.34, p < .001
- Perceived organisational support → psychological safety: β = 0.21, p < .01
- Evaluation apprehension → willingness to disclose: **β = -0.31, p < .001**
- Evaluation apprehension → psychological safety: β = -0.26, p < .001
- Perceived stigma → evaluation apprehension: β = 0.41, p < .001 (the single largest driver of apprehension)
- Perceived uncertainty → evaluation apprehension: β = 0.28, p < .001
- Privacy concern → evaluation apprehension: β = 0.24, p < .01

Notably, "psychological safety and willingness to disclose AI use were comparatively lower" than the concern-related constructs (perceived uncertainty, privacy concern, evaluation apprehension) across the full sample, meaning the default emotional posture toward disclosure in this population skews cautious rather than open.

Qualitative themes, with direct quotes:

- **Institutional clarity drives openness.** P07: disclosure is easier "if the teacher clearly explains how AI can be used, such as for brainstorming or checking language." P03: "students feel safer mentioning it because the rules are already clear" when instructors state expectations in the assignment brief. But P02: "some teachers encourage using AI as a learning tool, while others strongly discourage it": inconsistency across evaluators undermines the effect even for otherwise-willing students.
- **Stigma and ambiguity drive concealment.** P15: "even if AI only helps with ideas, teachers might assume the whole assignment was generated by AI." P09: "the rules about AI are not very stable yet, so it feels risky to talk about it openly." P18: "if you say you used AI, the teacher might check your assignment more carefully": disclosure is perceived to invite *more* scrutiny, not less.
- **Disclosure is strategic, not binary.** P20 would disclose minor assistance ("if it was only used to check grammar or organise ideas") but not substantial assistance. P14: "if the teacher is supportive, I might mention it, but otherwise I would rather not take the risk." P10, a generally pro-transparency student: "being honest about AI use sounds reasonable, but you still worry about how the teacher might interpret it." Students are running a live risk/benefit calculation on every disclosure decision, not applying a fixed personal policy.

## Why This Matters for Slackr

- Slackr's Track 1 research story concluded that the core problem isn't detecting unequal contribution, it's that *reporting* it carries a social cost, which is why the product reframed from "Contribution Radar" (an accusation mechanism) to "Automatic Contribution Evidence Report" (evidence, not verdict). This paper is the first study in the folder to put numbers behind that exact mechanism in an adjacent context: self-disclosure of AI-assisted work. Evaluation apprehension (fear of being judged) suppresses honest self-reporting with a similar-magnitude effect (β = -0.31) to how psychological safety enables it (β = 0.48), and the strongest single driver of that apprehension is stigma (β = 0.41), i.e. the perception that the disclosed behaviour will be read as illegitimate. That is structurally the same fear a student has admitting "I did less on this project" or "most of my contribution was pairing/verbal, not commits."
- Slackr's "Member context" field, where students add contribution the system can't observe (offline work, design discussions, coordination), is functionally a disclosure mechanism, and this paper says disclosure mechanisms only work if psychological safety is designed in, not assumed. Three concrete, transferable design levers fall out of the enabling pathway: perceived fairness (does the student believe the evidence pipeline treats all contribution types even-handedly), instructor/tutor support (is the framing supportive rather than investigative), and institutional clarity (do students know in advance what happens to what they write, and that it isn't graded automatically). Slackr's existing "review suggested, not decided" framing and human-in-the-loop tutor review already map onto the support and fairness levers; this paper suggests the product should also be explicit, in-product, about what "Member context" is and isn't used for, since policy ambiguity (β = 0.28 into evaluation apprehension) is a significant independent suppressor of honest input even when fairness and support are otherwise fine.
- The privacy-concern finding (β = 0.24 into evaluation apprehension, plus P18's "the teacher might check your assignment more carefully") is a direct warning for how Slackr should frame connecting GitHub/Docs/Meet: students may read "we now observe your activity" itself as an increase in scrutiny, independent of what the evidence actually shows, and that perception alone will suppress the voluntary, self-authored context that fills Slackr's biggest evidence gap (the Equity risk already flagged in [[2026-08-league-ethical-governance-student-data]]).
- Combined with [[2025-11-ai-contribution-conflict-framework]] and [[2026-06-github-accountability-gap-ai-attribution]], which cover how AI-assisted contribution complicates *attribution* of work already in the repo, this paper adds the missing upstream layer: whether a student will admit to AI assistance at all in the first place. If Slackr's evidence pipeline or report ever surfaces AI-tool usage as a contribution signal, this paper implies that signal will be systematically under-reported unless the same stigma/uncertainty/privacy conditions are addressed, which is a caution against treating any self-reported or inferred AI-usage field as ground truth.
- Methodological note for Slackr's own research story: the paper's CAC framework (cognition → affect → conation/behaviour) is a reusable lens for framing *why* the product's own qualitative interview findings (RQ5: "what information do tutors actually need," and the free-rider/social-conflict finding in [[2026-02-free-rider-emotions-team-reflexivity]]) work the way they do, i.e. it's not just that students fear conflict, it's a measurable cognition-affect chain that a product can intervene on at the cognitive-perception stage (fairness, clarity, support) rather than only at the behavioural stage.

This summary is compiled directly from the full HTML version of the paper (https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21733), including the abstract, introduction, hypotheses (H1-H9), Results section 6 (both quantitative SEM path coefficients and the three qualitative themes with interview quotes), and Discussion/Conclusion. All quotes and coefficients above are taken verbatim or as directly reported in that source. The paper is very recent (submitted 23 April 2026, v1) and does not yet appear in general web search indexes as a discussed/cited work, so this summary should be treated as the paper's own reported findings rather than corroborated by secondary commentary. Sample is limited to Chinese university students via convenience sampling, cross-sectional (no causal claims), and self-reported (social-desirability risk), all of which the authors themselves flag as limitations.
