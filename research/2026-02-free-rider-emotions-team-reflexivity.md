# Anticipation of Future Interaction and Emotions Toward Free Riders

**Authors:** Yi-Ching Liu (Department of Business Administration, National Taiwan University) and Hsu-Min Lee (postdoctoral fellow, National Taiwan University)
**Date:** First published online February 1, 2026
**Journal:** *Small Group Research* (SAGE), OnlineFirst
**Source:** [DOI: 10.1177/10464964261417691](https://doi.org/10.1177/10464964261417691) ([journal page](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10464964261417691))
**Keywords:** free-rider problem; team reflexivity; social emotion regulation; broaden-and-build theory; group decision-making
**Relevance to Slackr:** Directly on-topic. This is a lab experiment on the emotional and behavioral response teammates have to a free rider, and on what conditions turn that response constructive rather than punitive or avoidant. It gives Slackr a psychological mechanism, not just a survey statistic, for why evidence-based, low-conflict reporting is the right design and for why an "add missing context" step is worth building.

## Summary

The paper studies how teams emotionally and behaviorally respond to uneven contribution, and what makes that response constructive rather than corrosive. It applies broaden-and-build theory, normally used to explain how positive emotions expand behavior in positive settings, to the negative setting of working with an underperforming teammate. In a controlled lab experiment, participants were placed in teams with a confederate (a researcher posing as a participant) who was scripted to under-contribute. Two variables were manipulated: whether participants anticipated future interaction with the same team, and whether the team engaged in structured reflexivity (explicit reflection on and discussion of team process, not just task content).

## Key Findings

- Anticipating future collaboration with the same team increased sympathy toward the low-contributing member and increased willingness to keep interacting with them, rather than excluding or retaliating against them.
- Teams that engaged in reflexivity and information integration, meaning they explicitly discussed and pooled what each member knew about the situation, produced higher-quality group decisions than teams that did not.
- The authors frame this as evidence that positive emotions can "broaden" interpersonal openness and "build" the collective resources (trust, willingness to communicate, shared understanding) a team needs to keep functioning even while carrying a free rider.
- The 29-page article situates the free-rider response as an emotion-regulation and attribution problem: how a team appraises and feels about a low contributor shapes whether the team problem-solves around it or fractures over it, independent of the underlying contribution gap itself.

## Why This Matters for Slackr

- This gives an experimental, causal complement to the survey-based findings in [[2026-07-students-perceptions-peer-grading]]. That paper documents that students fear conflict and damaged relationships when reporting unequal contribution; this paper shows a lever that reduces that fear: when people expect to keep working together, their emotional reaction to a free rider shifts toward sympathy and continued engagement rather than punishment. That is a direct argument for Slackr's choice to generate the Contribution Report as passive, ongoing evidence collection rather than a one-time accusatory act, since it does not force a rupture point the way an active peer report does.
- The reflexivity finding, structured reflection and information-pooling producing better decisions, is empirical support for Slackr's "Add Missing Context" step in the MVP flow. It suggests that step should not just be a free-text box appended to raw evidence; framing it as a structured prompt for the team (or the individual) to integrate context, similar to a reflexivity intervention, is more likely to produce a decision (here, the instructor's read of the report) that reflects real understanding of what happened rather than a surface reading of activity counts.
- Because the paper's manipulation is anticipated future interaction, it also implies a design caution: Slackr reports are typically generated at project end, when future interaction with this exact team may be low or zero. That is precisely the condition under which this study's data would predict less sympathy and more punitive reactions toward a low contributor. This argues for keeping the instructor, rather than teammates, as the audience for the final report's judgment call, consistent with the "evidence, not verdict" principle in the Slackr MVP doc, since removing peer-to-peer confrontation from the end-of-project moment sidesteps the exact condition under which this research shows responses turn less constructive.
- Combined with [[2025-11-ai-contribution-conflict-framework]] (instructor oversight is the most load-bearing mitigation across negative peer-grading themes), this paper adds the underlying psychological reason why: instructor review substitutes for the "anticipated future interaction" effect the study identifies, providing a stable, low-stakes evaluator relationship in place of a fraught peer-to-peer one that may never recur.
