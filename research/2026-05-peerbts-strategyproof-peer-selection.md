# PeerBTS: Incentivizing Effort in Strategyproof Peer Selection

**Authors:** Harper Lyon (Tulane University), Omer Lev (Ben-Gurion University), Nicholas Mattei (Tulane University)
**Date:** Submitted 22 May 2026 (arXiv preprint); appears at the 8th Games, Agents, and Incentives Workshop (GAIW-26), held as part of the Workshops at the 25th International Conference on Autonomous Agents and Multiagent Systems (AAMAS), May 2026, Paphos, Cyprus
**Source:** [arXiv:2605.24165](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24165) (HTML: https://arxiv.org/html/2605.24165v1, PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.24165v1)
**Relevance to Slackr:** Directly on-topic. This is a mechanism-design paper about peer evaluation/peer selection (grading in MOOCs, academic peer review, and by extension any setting where a group reports on or ranks its own members), and it proves a formal impossibility result that is directly relevant to Slackr's core design choice: any peer-reporting mechanism that only takes people's evaluations of each other as input can never simultaneously (a) be fair/strategyproof and (b) actually reward people for putting honest effort into giving that evaluation. This is a theoretical, game-theoretic complement to the empirical "peer grading is emotionally costly and gameable" evidence already collected in this folder.

## Summary

The paper studies peer selection: the process by which a group of agents (students, conference authors, grant applicants) evaluate and select some subset of their own members, e.g. MOOC students grading each other's work, or conference authors who must also review other submissions. Existing research in this space has focused heavily on making these mechanisms strategyproof, meaning no agent can improve their own odds of being selected by lying about their evaluation of others. The authors point out a flaw shared by essentially all published strategyproof mechanisms: none of them reward an agent for the effort of producing a genuinely careful, high-quality evaluation in the first place. When evaluating others is costly (reading a paper, actually assessing a teammate's work) rather than free, a purely strategyproof mechanism gives agents zero incentive to try hard, because by design their own evaluation of others can never affect whether they themselves get selected.

The paper's central theoretical contribution is a proof that this is not a fixable implementation detail but a structural impossibility: no anonymous, strategyproof (or even the weaker "incentive-compatible") peer-evaluation mechanism can motivate agents to produce truthful, high-effort evaluations if the only information collected from each agent is their evaluation itself. To escape this impossibility, the authors propose PeerBTS, which layers a peer-prediction "lottery" (built on the Robust Bayesian Truth Serum, a mechanism from the forecasting/survey-scoring literature) on top of an existing strategyproof selection mechanism (they instantiate it with PeerNomination). Concretely, PeerBTS asks each reviewer for two things instead of one: their evaluation of the peers they're assigned to review, and a prediction of how the other reviewers of that same peer will evaluate them. Reviewers are then scored and rewarded (via a lottery-style bonus, separate from the main selection outcome) based on how well their prediction matches what other reviewers actually said, using a proper scoring rule that makes truthful, effortful reporting the reviewer's own best strategy. The paper proves this reward layer can be added while keeping the overall system Bayes-Nash incentive compatible, runs non-strategic simulations comparing PeerBTS to existing strategyproof mechanisms, and reports an initial validity study of the peer-prediction component using data from a small academic workshop.

## Key Findings

- **Existing strategyproof peer-evaluation mechanisms structurally cannot reward effort (Theorem 1).** If a mechanism is anonymous and strategyproof (or even just incentive-compatible), and the only input it takes from an agent is that agent's evaluation of others, then that agent's chance of being selected can never depend on how much effort they put into their own evaluation. Since producing a high-quality, truthful evaluation is assumed to be costly, a rational effort-minimizing agent has no reason to pay that cost.
- **Corollary: incentivizing effort requires an extra signal beyond the evaluation itself.** Any mechanism that is anonymous, strategyproof/incentive-compatible, and effort-incentivizing must collect at least one additional piece of information from agents beyond their raw evaluation, which motivates PeerBTS's design of also collecting predictions.
- **"Torpedo reviewing" is named as a concrete real-world failure mode of naive strategyproof-adjacent systems.** In a conference where a submission's grade is the average of its reviewers' scores and only the top-k are accepted, every reviewer is individually better off scoring all other submissions as 0, since this cannot hurt their own paper but can only lower the bar needed for their own paper to clear the top-k cutoff. This behavior has been documented at real conferences.
- **PeerBTS's fix: pay agents (via a separate lottery/scoring mechanism) for accurately predicting how their fellow reviewers will rate the same target**, not for matching the target's "true" score. This is deliberately not the same as older approaches that reward agents for guessing the consensus rating directly, which the authors note is not strategyproof, since an agent who privately spots a real flaw is then incentivized to suppress it and just report what they expect everyone else to say.
- **The mechanism is general-purpose**: PeerBTS is presented as a wrapper that can combine the Robust Bayesian Truth Serum reward layer with *any* existing strategyproof or incentive-compatible peer-selection mechanism, not just PeerNomination, which the authors use only as a concrete worked example.
- The paper validates the approach with non-strategic simulations against existing strategyproof baselines and an initial empirical check of peer-prediction validity using real reviewer data from a small academic workshop; the numeric results of these evaluations were not accessible in the excerpt fetched for this note (see Sourcing note below).

## Notable Quotes

> "The problem with strategyproof mechanisms is that, by definition, they force a separation between the evaluations an agent gives on others and the possibility of that same agent being selected."

> "When there is a cost - whether of time, effort, or monetary value (as with reviewing papers, grant proposals, or Coursera student work) - the disconnect between evaluations and agents' personal benefit is detrimental to the quality of the peer evaluation since there is no within mechanism incentive to exert effort."

> "All reviewers will be better off giving 0 as their grade to anyone, as while they cannot affect the grade their submission receives, they can lower the threshold grade to be in the top-k, so their work might be selected. This is typically called 'torpedo reviewing' in the literature and has been documented at conferences."

> "A peer-evaluation mechanism cannot be anonymous and strategyproof (or incentive-compatible) and incentivize agents to produce a high quality (i.e., truthful) [evaluation], if its input is only [that evaluation]." (Theorem 1, paraphrased for readability)

> Reward schemes that pay agents "for giving a review close to the average of others agents' reviews... are inherently not strategyproof, as agents are being rewarded not for giving their truthful views, but for being able to predict consensus opinion."

## Why This Matters for Slackr

- This paper's impossibility result gives formal, game-theoretic backing to a design instinct Slackr already has: a peer-reporting system that only collects "what did you think of your teammate's contribution" and uses that directly to influence outcomes cannot be trusted to produce honest, effortful reports, no matter how the scoring math is arranged. This strengthens the case (alongside [[2026-07-students-perceptions-peer-grading]] and [[2025-11-ai-contribution-conflict-framework]]) for Slackr sourcing contribution evidence from artifacts, commits, docs, meeting logs, rather than asking students to actively rate each other.
- The "torpedo reviewing" failure mode is a concrete, named illustration of the same underlying risk flagged more informally as "retaliation/collusion/strategic grading" in [[2026-07-students-perceptions-peer-grading]] and "students can game the system with contradictory or misleading submissions" in [[2025-11-ai-contribution-conflict-framework]]. This paper explains *why* that gaming is not just a behavioral quirk but a rational best-response under common mechanism designs, which is a stronger, more citable framing for Slackr's background research than the behavioral evidence alone.
- The core problem PeerBTS solves, i.e. how do you get people to genuinely try when reporting on peers instead of taking the path of least resistance, is exactly the problem Slackr sidesteps by design: because Slackr's evidence pipeline does not depend on students actively scoring or ranking each other, it is not exposed to the specific impossibility this paper proves. That makes this paper useful primarily as a rigorous articulation of the failure mode Slackr avoids, worth citing as "here is a formal proof of why peer-rating-based systems face a structural honesty/effort problem that evidence-based systems do not."
- If Slackr ever does add any lightweight peer-input feature (e.g. asking a teammate to confirm or add context to an automatically surfaced piece of evidence, as flagged as a design consideration in [[2026-07-students-perceptions-peer-grading]]), this paper's core insight, that meaningful engagement needs a reward signal separate from the outcome the input is used for, is a useful constraint to keep in mind: a "confirm/dispute" input bolted directly onto a grading outcome would inherit the same effort-incentive problem this paper proves exists.
